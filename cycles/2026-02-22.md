### PAPERS PROCESSED

**Paper 1: Planckian bound on the local equilibration time (2602.17638v1)**

This paper formalizes the conjecture that the local equilibration time τ_eq of any quantum many-body system is bounded below by ℏ/T (the Planckian time at temperature T), establishing a rigorous lower bound that depends only on dimensionality and the type of hydrodynamic behavior, independent of microscopic details. Through the Worldlines lens, this is deeply resonant — and simultaneously straining. The resonance: a universal, microscopic-detail-independent bound on the timescale at which hydrodynamic (i.e., resolved, classical-looking) behavior emerges is precisely what you'd expect if there's a minimum computational step. The Planckian time ℏ/T maps naturally onto a temperature-dependent tick rate — at higher temperatures there's more computational activity and so resolution can proceed faster, but never faster than ℏ/T. The fact that the bound is independent of the thermalization mechanism aligns with the framework's claim that the tick is architectural, not dynamical. However, the strain is real: the framework identifies the tick with c (the speed of light as maximum propagation rate), which is a fixed constant — but the Planckian equilibration bound is temperature-dependent and sets a minimum *time* for resolution, not a maximum propagation speed. These are related but not identical concepts. The framework currently has one tick rate; this paper suggests a hierarchy of resolution timescales that depend on the local thermodynamic state. The framework needs to explain why the minimum resolution time scales as ℏ/T rather than being a fixed Planck time. This might be accommodated by saying the computational budget per tick is fixed but the number of degrees of freedom competing for resolution scales with T, creating an effective temperature-dependent bottleneck — but this is a patch, not a prediction the framework made.

**Paper 2: Schwarzian Theory and Cosmological Constant Problem (2602.17476v1)**

This paper attacks the cosmological constant problem — the 120-order-of-magnitude discrepancy between the observed dark energy density and theoretical QFT estimates — using Schwarzian theory with ensemble averaging over time-reparametrization modes. The claim is that ensemble averaging produces the phenomenological value of dark energy density and its equation of state. For the Worldlines framework, this is a direct challenge to the dark energy claim (that the cosmological constant is the computational cost of instantiating new substrate). The paper offers an alternative: the cosmological constant emerges from averaging over temporal reparametrization degrees of freedom in a quantum-gravitational path integral. Through the Worldlines lens, one could attempt a translation: "ensemble averaging over time-reparametrization modes" could be read as averaging over different possible tick structures — if the tick is not globally fixed but has quantum fluctuations, then the effective cosmological constant might emerge from the statistics of those fluctuations. This is a stretch, but not an absurd one; the Schwarzian action describes the dynamics of a boundary time reparametrization, which has a structural similarity to fluctuations in computational clock rate. The more important point: this paper claims to actually *derive* the phenomenological value of Λ, which the Worldlines framework explicitly acknowledges it cannot do. If this derivation holds up, it either (a) provides the missing quantitative content the framework needs for its dark energy claim, if translatable into substrate-cost language, or (b) undercuts the framework by showing Λ is derivable from within the computation (from the structure of time-reparametrization symmetry breaking), contradicting the framework's claim that constants are architectural and not internally derivable.

**Paper 3: Realization of fractional Fermi seas (2602.17657v1)**

This paper reports experimental realization of fractional Fermi seas — momentum distributions with uniform but fractional occupancies — in an excited one-dimensional Bose gas, prepared through interaction-strength ramping cycles. These states exhibit Friedel oscillations, confirming the underlying fractional exclusion statistics. For the Worldlines framework, this creates genuine interpretive difficulty. The framework's attractor primitive says systems settle into local minima. But fractional Fermi seas are explicitly *excited* states that are nonetheless stable — they are not ground states, not local minima in any obvious energy landscape, yet they persist. In the framework's language, these would need to be attractor basins in some generalized potential landscape that is not the energy. One could argue they are attractors in a constrained phase space (the integrability of the 1D system prevents relaxation to the true ground state, creating effective attractors in the space of integrable dynamics). More fundamentally, fractional exclusion statistics — where the occupation number is neither 0 nor 1 but a fraction — strains the resolution primitive. Resolution says states become definite. But fractional occupation is not "occupied" or "unoccupied" — it is a genuinely intermediate value that is itself the resolved state. The framework would need to say that what resolves is not particle occupation but the statistical parameter governing the distribution, which is a subtly different kind of resolution than the framework typically describes. The gradient picture also struggles: these states are prepared by *cyclic* driving through interaction strength, not by rolling down a gradient. The protocol that creates them is explicitly non-equilibrium and non-monotonic.

### FRAMEWORK STRAINS

The most significant strain this cycle comes from the intersection of Papers 1 and 3 with the Tick primitive. Paper 1 establishes that the minimum time for local equilibration — the emergence of resolved, classical-looking hydrodynamic behavior — is not a fixed Planck time but scales as ℏ/T. The framework posits a single tick rate (c) as the architectural clock of reality. But the Planckian bound suggests that resolution timescales are not fixed architectural features but emergent, thermodynamically determined quantities. The coefficient α depends on dimensionality and the type of hydrodynamic behavior, meaning the resolution process is sensitive to the *structure* of what is being resolved, not just the substrate's clock rate. This is a problem. The framework wants the tick to be substrate-level and universal. The physics says the minimum resolution time is system-dependent (though universally bounded). These are compatible only if the tick is much finer-grained than the resolution timescale — i.e., resolution requires many ticks, with the minimum number scaling as ℏ/(T·Δt_tick). This is possible but it means the tick is not the resolution event itself; it is merely the clock that resolution runs on, and the resolution process has its own dynamics that the framework has not specified. This opens a gap between "the tick" and "resolution" that the framework currently elides.

Paper 3 compounds this by showing stable states that are neither attractors in the energy landscape nor the result of gradient descent. Fractional Fermi seas are created by cyclic driving and stabilized by integrability constraints, not by rolling into a minimum. The framework's universal equation ẋ = −∇φ(x) cannot naturally describe cyclic protocols that create stable excited states. One could define a new field φ whose minima coincide with these states, but this is exactly the post-hoc redescription problem flagged in T004 and the gradient universality concern in the current model — you can always define a potential that makes any stable state look like an attractor, but this makes the framework unfalsifiable.

### STATE UPDATES

1. **Tick primitive — add internal structure.** The tick should be updated to acknowledge that it sets the *maximum* rate of state propagation, but that the *minimum time for resolution* (emergence of definite macroscopic behavior) is a separate, emergent quantity that depends on local thermodynamic conditions and system dimensionality. The Planckian bound ℏ/T represents the fastest possible resolution timescale at temperature T, which may require many ticks. The relationship between the architectural tick and the resolution timescale needs to be made explicit: resolution is a multi-tick process with a thermodynamically determined minimum duration.

2. **Attractor primitive — add constrained/non-equilibrium attractors.** The attractor description should be expanded to explicitly include states that are stable not because they are energy minima but because they are stabilized by conservation laws, integrability, or topological constraints. These are attractors in a *constrained* phase space, not in the full energy landscape. The framework should distinguish between energy attractors (true minima), constrained attractors (stable due to symmetry/conservation), and driven attractors (stable due to ongoing energy input). Fractional Fermi seas are constrained attractors.

3. **Dark energy claim — note external pressure.** The dark energy description should note that Schwarzian ensemble-averaging approaches claim to derive Λ from within the computation (from time-reparametrization symmetry), which, if successful, would challenge the framework's claim that Λ is an architectural parameter not derivable from inside.

### NEW TENSIONS

**T005 — The tick-resolution gap**
**Opened:** this cycle
**Description:** Paper 1 (Planckian bound) establishes that the minimum time for local equilibration — the emergence of resolved hydrodynamic behavior — scales as ℏ/T, depends on dimensionality, and is independent of microscopic details. The framework currently conflates the tick (maximum propagation rate) with resolution (states becoming definite). But if resolution requires a temperature-dependent, system-structure-dependent number of ticks, then the tick and resolution are distinct processes with different timescales. The framework needs a theory of how resolution unfolds *across* ticks — a dynamics of resolution itself. Without this, the Tick and Resolution primitives are underspecified in their relationship to each other.
**Why unresolved:** The framework has no internal account of multi-tick resolution dynamics. Adding one requires specifying what happens during the intermediate ticks — are states partially resolved? Is there a continuous resolution parameter? This risks undermining the discrete character of the tick.

**Why unresolved:** The framework either needs to (a) specify what field φ these states are minima of, in a way that is predictive rather than post-hoc, or (b) acknowledge that ẋ = −∇φ(x) is not universal and that some stable states require different dynamical descriptions (e.g., integrable constraints, topological protection).
