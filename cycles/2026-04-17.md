**Paper 1: "Taming the Aretakis instability: extremal black holes with multi-degenerate horizons"**
ArXiv ID: 2604.15260v1 | Selection type: Strain

Through the Worldlines lens, extremal black holes are attractor basins at a critical boundary — the point where the attractor's depth exactly matches the tick rate (escape velocity = c), with zero surface gravity meaning the attractor has a degenerate lip rather than a sharp edge. The Aretakis instability is a phenomenon where perturbations at this degenerate boundary grow polynomially along the horizon generators — the attractor's rim is dynamically unstable even though it is an exact equilibrium. The framework would describe this as: the attractor's boundary is a saddle point where resolution dynamics are pathological. The paper's central finding — that *increasing* the order of horizon degeneracy *weakens* the instability, and that an infinitely degenerate horizon may be stable — strains the framework in a specific and novel way. In the framework's language, making the attractor's lip "more degenerate" (flatter) should make it *more* unstable (shallower saddle, easier to perturb away). Instead, the opposite happens: infinite degeneracy produces stability. The "graveyard" end state is an attractor basin whose boundary has been smoothed to infinite flatness, and this infinite flatness produces stability rather than instability. The framework's Attractor primitive has no mechanism for relating the *order of degeneracy* at a saddle/boundary to the *stability* of perturbations there. More fundamentally, the stability analysis requires the concept of *Aretakis charges* — conserved quantities on the horizon that are horizon-specific, gauge-invariant, and encode the transverse derivative structure of the perturbation at the degenerate surface. These are conserved quantities that exist *only* on the attractor boundary, not in the bulk computation, and their vanishing (for the infinitely degenerate case) is what produces stability. The framework's Conservation primitive describes bulk conservation; horizon-localized charges with no bulk analogue represent a new category of conservation structure the framework cannot express. The relationship between horizon degeneracy order and Aretakis charge structure is algebraic/geometric (determined by the near-horizon expansion of the metric function), not dynamical — reinforcing T059 (ontological inversion). The paper also connects to T067/T097 (resolution regularity): the Aretakis instability involves derivatives of the field blowing up at the horizon while the field itself remains bounded, meaning the instability is visible only at a specific regularity class (C¹ but not C²), and the "stable graveyard" has regularity at all finite derivative orders — the resolution regularity hierarchy is physically decisive here.

### FRAMEWORK STRAINS

The most significant strain this cycle comes from Paper 1's demonstration that the stability of extremal black hole horizons is *inversely* related to their degeneracy order, culminating in the proposal that an infinitely degenerate horizon is dynamically stable — a "graveyard" end state. This strains the framework at the Attractor primitive specifically.

The framework describes black holes as "attractor basins so deep that escape velocity exceeds the tick rate." Extremal black holes are the limiting case where escape velocity *exactly equals* the tick rate — the attractor's lip is exactly at the threshold. The framework's intuition — informed by the Field primitive's gradient dynamics — is that a "flatter" lip (higher degeneracy order) should be *less* stable: perturbations on a flatter potential surface should persist longer and grow more easily. Paper 1 demonstrates the precise opposite: the flatter the lip, the weaker the instability, and infinite flatness produces stability.

The mechanism is structural, not dynamical: the Aretakis charges (conserved quantities localized on the horizon) become increasingly constrained as degeneracy increases. For an n-th order degenerate horizon, only the first (n-1) transverse derivatives of the field contribute to the Aretakis charges, and the polynomial growth rate of the instability decreases. At infinite degeneracy, the Aretakis charges vanish identically, and the instability is absent. The stability is *not* produced by the attractor being deeper or wider — it is produced by the *algebraic structure of horizon-localized conservation laws* changing with degeneracy order. The framework's Attractor primitive describes geometry (depth, width, saddle structure); the relevant physics is algebra (derivative order of horizon charges). The Conservation primitive describes bulk conserved quantities; the relevant quantities exist only on the attractor boundary.

This is a clean case where the framework's dynamical intuition (flatter → less stable) is contradicted by the structural reality (flatter → more stable), with the mechanism residing in algebraic structure (horizon-localized charges, degeneracy order) that no primitive can express. The framework cannot even formulate the question "what happens to the Aretakis instability as horizon degeneracy increases?" because it has no concept of horizon degeneracy order as a physical parameter.
