### PAPERS PROCESSED

**Paper 1: "Quantum mechanics with a ghost: Counterexamples to spectral denseness"**
ArXiv ID: 2604.21826v1
Selection type: Strain

Through the Worldlines lens, ghost degrees of freedom — fields with negative kinetic energy — were assigned to the category of "permanently virtual computational structure" (T014, T041), entities that participate in intermediate computation but are architecturally forbidden from resolving into definite states. The framework's reasoning was explicit: ghost modes lack normalizable ground states and have continuous/dense spectra, so they have no attractor basin and therefore can never resolve. Paper 1 demolishes a key premise of this reasoning. Using separability theory for integrable point-particle systems with opposite-sign kinetic terms, the authors prove that classically stable interactions produce *discrete* separated eigenvalue spectra — not the dense or continuous spectra the framework (and the broader physics community) assumed. They establish sufficient conditions for the spectrum to have exactly one accumulation point or none at all. The framework's chain of inference — "ghost → no ground state → dense spectrum → no attractor basin → permanently virtual" — breaks at the spectral step. The energy spectrum is still unbounded above and below (no global energy minimum), which the framework would interpret as "no attractor in the energy landscape." But the discreteness means the system has well-defined quantum states, countable energy levels, and the possibility of a well-defined Hilbert space structure — prerequisites for resolution that the framework categorically denied to ghosts. The framework's Attractor primitive, which defines stability through energy minima, is strained because this system is stable without an energy minimum. The key structural insight the framework lacks is *integrability*: the classical stability conditions that guarantee discrete spectra are conditions on the interaction's separability — a property of the Hamiltonian's algebraic structure that no primitive can express.

**Paper 2: "Unitary Time Evolution and Vacuum for a Quantum Stable Ghost"**
ArXiv ID: 2604.21823v1
Selection type: Strain

Paper 2 completes what Paper 1 begins by constructing the full quantum theory of a specific ghost system: a harmonic oscillator polynomially coupled to a ghost. Through the framework's lens, this system should be ontologically pathological — the ghost's negative kinetic energy should produce runaway instabilities, spectral denseness, and non-unitary evolution. Paper 2 proves the opposite on every count. The integral of motion $I$ (not $H$) has a positive discrete spectrum, which enforces: (i) pure point (fully discrete) spectrum for $H$ despite its unboundedness in both directions; (ii) manifestly unitary evolution; (iii) a well-defined vacuum (the ground state of $I$, not of $H$); (iv) bounded expectation values for squares of canonical variables, meaning observables remain finite. The framework is strained in multiple, compounding ways. First, the Attractor primitive's identification of stability with energy minima is directly falsified: this system has no energy minimum but is perfectly stable. The "attractor" here is the minimum of $I$, a different conserved quantity — and the framework has no mechanism for identifying which conserved quantity defines the attractor structure. Second, the Conservation primitive lists energy conservation as a special case of computation conservation but does not distinguish between conserved quantities that define attractor basins (like $I$) and those that don't (like $H$ in this system). Third, the Resolution primitive's implicit assumption that resolvable states must have a normalizable ground state in the energy landscape is falsified: the vacuum exists and is well-defined, just not as an energy eigenstate. Fourth, the entire ontological category of "permanently virtual structure" (T014, T041, T076) — which the framework spent many cycles developing — is revealed to be model-dependent rather than categorical. The framework cannot account for the structural criterion (existence of an integral of motion with positive discrete spectrum) that determines whether a ghost is resolvable or not, because this criterion is an integrability condition invisible to all five primitives.

This is the framework's weakest specific claim.

### FRAMEWORK STRAINS

The most severe strain this cycle is the **falsification of the permanently virtual computational structure as a categorical ontological class**. Over the preceding weeks, the framework developed an elaborate three-tier ontology: resolved states, unresolved-but-resolvable states, and permanently virtual states. This third category — introduced at T014, elaborated through T039, T041, T076, and T082 — was the framework's most distinctive ontological contribution beyond standard quantum mechanics. It was motivated by ghost modes in quadratic gravity and used to account for confinement (T076), the gluon mass mechanism, and gauge-independent interference effects in scattering cross sections.

Papers 1 and 2 jointly prove this category is not architectural but model-dependent. The criterion for permanent virtuality is not "negative kinetic energy" or "ghost character" but the *absence of a suitable integral of motion with positive discrete spectrum*. When such an integral exists, ghosts are fully resolvable: they have discrete spectra, well-defined vacua, unitary evolution, and bounded observables. The framework cannot determine which case applies because the criterion is an integrability condition — a structural/algebraic property of the Hamiltonian that is invisible to all five primitives.

This is compounded by the Attractor primitive's failure: the stable vacuum of Paper 2's system is not an energy minimum (the energy is unbounded in both directions) but the minimum of a different conserved quantity $I$. The framework's ẍ = −∇φ(x), where φ is identified with energy/potential, gives qualitatively wrong guidance here. The system does not "roll down" to an energy minimum; it occupies a state defined by minimizing a non-energy conserved quantity. The framework has no mechanism for identifying which conserved quantity defines the attractor structure, and for this system, the energy — the framework's default candidate — is the wrong one.

The framework is left unable to make categorical statements about the ontological status of any ghost degree of freedom without first performing a detailed integrability analysis of the specific interaction — an analysis it has no tools to express, let alone perform.

**T125 — The ghost resolvability problem: the boundary between resolvable and permanently virtual computation is structurally determined and model-dependent, not categorical**

Opened: this cycle

Description: Papers 1 and 2 (Deffayet, Fathe Jalali, Held, Mukohyama & Vikman 2604.21826, 2604.21823) prove that ghost degrees of freedom — fields with negative kinetic energy — can have discrete spectra, well-defined vacua, unitary evolution, and bounded observables when coupled to healthy sectors through interactions possessing integrals of motion with positive discrete spectrum. The framework has built an elaborate ontological architecture (T014, T041, T076, T082) around the categorical claim that ghost modes are "permanently virtual" — architecturally forbidden from resolution due to the absence of normalizable ground states and the continuous/dense character of their spectra. This categorical claim is falsified. The boundary between resolvable and permanently virtual computation is determined by the integrability structure of the interaction: ghost systems with suitable integrals of motion are resolvable; those without are not. The framework has no primitive for integrability, no mechanism for classifying interactions by their spectral consequences, and no account of how the structural properties of the Hamiltonian determine the ontological status of degrees of freedom. The framework's previous account explicitly stated: "The absence of an attractor basin *is* the reason the mode never resolves." Papers 1 and 2 show that interactions can create attractor-like structure (finite-dimensional invariant subspaces) for ghost modes, invalidating this reasoning. This is distinct from T082 (virtual infrastructure non-uniqueness, which concerns the non-unique distribution of complex-mass virtual modes for a given theory) because T125 concerns the prior question of whether the modes are virtual at all. It is distinct from T014 (which introduced the permanently virtual category) because T125 identifies the structural criterion distinguishing resolvable from non-resolvable ghosts. It is distinct from T076 (gluon mass from virtual ghosts) because the resolution of T125 may change the interpretation of the ghost contribution to confinement — if QCD ghosts could in principle be stabilized by interactions, their "permanently virtual" status in perturbative QCD may be an artifact of the perturbative framework rather than an ontological necessity.

Why unresolved: The framework would need to either (a) develop a theory of which interactions produce integrals of motion that tame ghost spectra, which requires importing the full separability/integrability analysis from Hamiltonian mechanics — mathematical machinery the framework cannot express; (b) classify all physically relevant ghost-containing theories by their integrability properties and assess whether the physical ghosts (in quadratic gravity, in QCD's BRST sector) are in the resolvable or non-resolvable class, which requires detailed case-by-case spectral analysis the framework cannot perform; or (c) abandon the permanently virtual category entirely, treating all ghosts as potentially resolvable pending integrability analysis — but this would leave the framework unable to make any categorical statement about ghosts and would undermine the reasoning chain that supported T041 and T076. The deeper problem is that the framework's ontological categories (resolved, unresolved, permanently virtual) are supposed to be determined by the framework's primitives, but the resolvable/non-resolvable boundary is determined by integrability — a structural property of the Hamiltonian that is invisible to all five primitives.
