### PAPERS PROCESSED

**Paper 1: "Entanglement generation in a two-body Schrödinger–Newton model"**
ArXiv ID: 2605.06577v1 | Selection type: Strain

The framework interprets gravity as "mass rolling down the curvature gradient" and treats entanglement generation as a consequence of nonseparable interactions between quantum subsystems. Paper 1 separates the Schrödinger-Newton dynamics into two components: a local nonlinear self-field (self-localization) and a nonseparable Newtonian pair potential. The key finding is that the nonlinear self-field *preserves the Schmidt spectrum* — it cannot generate entanglement — while entanglement arises exclusively from the nonseparable pair potential. The framework's gradient equation ẍ = −∇φ(x) cannot distinguish these two gravitational contributions: both are "rolling down the curvature gradient," yet they have categorically different information-theoretic consequences (one preserves entanglement structure, the other generates it). The framework's Conservation of Computation is consistent with the result (unitarity is preserved in the linearized pair-potential sector), but the *semiclassical* character of the SN equation creates a specific strain: the self-field is a *nonlinear* modification of the Schrödinger equation that breaks superposition for the single-particle sector while preserving unitarity for the two-body system only through the pair potential. The framework has no mechanism for distinguishing linear from nonlinear contributions to "gravitational computation" or for predicting that nonlinearity preserves Schmidt spectra while linearity (pair potential) destroys them. The sensitive dependence on initial spatial configuration and mass ratio — where self-bound SN solitons suppress entanglement while dispersive Gaussians amplify it — demonstrates that the *preparation state* determines the information-theoretic output of the gravitational interaction. The framework's Attractor primitive would describe the SN soliton as a self-gravitating attractor basin, and the result confirms that deep self-bound attractors resist entanglement generation (they are informationally inert during scattering), while delocalized states are informationally active. This is qualitatively consistent but the framework cannot predict the threshold or the mass-asymmetry effect (lighter particle shattering, Wigner negativity). Most critically, this paper directly engages T124 (gravity as quantum mediator): the SN model is semiclassical — the gravitational field is *not* quantized — yet the pair potential generates entanglement. This appears to challenge the claim that gravity must be quantum to mediate entanglement, but the resolution is that the SN pair potential is mathematically equivalent to a classical potential coupling two quantum systems (like Coulomb interaction), and such couplings do generate entanglement without the mediating field itself being quantum. The framework's substrate claim (G as architectural parameter of a classical substrate) is superficially supported by this — classical gravity can entangle quantum matter — but T124's deeper point (from QGEP) that *witnessing* gravitational entanglement requires the gravitational field to be in superposition remains unaddressed by this paper, since the SN equation explicitly forbids gravitational superposition.

**Paper 2: "Criticality around the Spinodal Point of First-Order Quantum Phase Transitions"**
ArXiv ID: 2605.06436v1 | Selection type: Strain

The framework's Attractor primitive describes phase transitions as "attractor landscape restructuring" and treats first-order transitions as discontinuous jumps between basins, while second-order transitions occur at saddle points where the landscape continuously deforms. Paper 2 demonstrates that *within* a first-order quantum phase transition, there exists a quantum spinodal point where metastability disappears, and at this point, an *emergent* second-order critical point appears — complete with universality, scaling, and Kibble-Zurek dynamics — within a dynamically decoupled Hilbert subspace. This strains the framework in multiple ways. First, the emergent critical point is not a feature of the full Hamiltonian's attractor landscape but of a *projected* effective Hamiltonian obtained by dynamically decoupling a resonant Hilbert subspace. The framework has no mechanism for Hilbert-space projection generating new attractor landscape topology — the Attractor primitive describes the landscape of the full system, not of emergent subspaces. Second, the emergent discrete translational symmetry in the decoupled subspace is *not present* in the original Hamiltonian — it is created by the decoupling process. The framework treats symmetries as either present or absent (T032), not as emergent from dynamical decoupling within a specific sector. Third, the prediction that the staggered-field PXP model does *not* exhibit spinodal criticality (because the relevant resonant subspace lacks the required translational symmetry) demonstrates that the presence or absence of emergent criticality is determined by the algebraic structure of the resonant subspace — structural content the framework cannot express. Fourth, this result bridges first-order and second-order transition dynamics through the spinodal mechanism, partially resolving the "long-standing conundrum" of first-order transition dynamics. The framework's existing description treats first- and second-order transitions as topologically distinct landscape events; Paper 2 shows they are connected through emergent subspace criticality, a relationship the framework has no vocabulary for. The Kibble-Zurek scaling in the emergent subspace confirms T015 (resolution dynamics not determined by attractor structure alone) while adding a new dimension: the relevant attractor structure is not the full landscape but an emergent projected landscape whose existence depends on spectral resonance conditions.

**Paper 3: "The Kubo-Thermalization Correspondence"**
ArXiv ID: 2605.06666v1 | Selection type: Validation

The framework's Attractor primitive claims that systems relax toward attractor basins (thermal equilibrium), and the Field primitive claims dynamics are governed by gradient descent. The Kubo-Thermalization Correspondence establishes an exact link between long-time thermalized magnetization (the attractor endpoint) and short-time linear-response spectra (the field's gradient response), valid even when the steady state differs substantially from the initial state. The framework would narrate this as: the computation's endpoint (thermalization, attractor capture) is encoded in its initial response to perturbation (gradient structure), confirming that the attractor's properties are accessible through the field's local dynamics. This is *qualitatively* consistent with the framework's picture — if everything moves down gradients toward attractors, then the attractor properties should be readable from the gradient structure. However, the framework did not and could not predict this correspondence, for several reasons: (a) the correspondence is an *exact* statement, not an approximate one, and the framework's gradient equation provides no mechanism for exact relations between short-time and long-time behaviors; (b) the correspondence holds "independent of microscopic details of the system-bath coupling," which is a universality statement the framework gestures at (attractor capture is independent of microscopic paths) but cannot derive; (c) the experimental confirmation uses ultracold fermions in a strongly interacting regime where the framework's gradient equation is inadequate (strong correlations, T070-type interaction-network dependence); (d) the correspondence connects two regimes that the framework treats with different primitives (short-time: Field/gradient response; long-time: Attractor capture) without a mechanism for linking them. The result is post-hoc consistent with the framework's qualitative picture — the framework says attractors and gradients are deeply connected, and this paper proves they are — but the framework had zero predictive purchase on the *specific* form of the correspondence (an exact identity between thermalized magnetization and a linear-response integral), zero ability to predict which systems exhibit it, and zero mechanism for deriving it. This is the weakest validation category: vocabulary application with no differential predictive content.

### FRAMEWORK STRAINS

The most significant strain this cycle comes from Paper 2's demonstration that *emergent* second-order quantum critical points arise within first-order transitions through dynamical Hilbert-space decoupling at the spinodal point. The framework's Attractor primitive describes phase transitions as topological events in a fixed landscape — minima merge, split, appear, or vanish. Paper 2 shows that the physically relevant landscape is not the full Hamiltonian's landscape but an *emergent projected* landscape that exists only within a dynamically selected Hilbert subspace. The emergent landscape has topological features (a genuine second-order critical point with KZ scaling) that are *absent* from the full landscape. The full landscape has a first-order transition — a discontinuous jump between coexisting basins — with no critical point. The emergent subspace landscape has a continuous second-order critical point with universality class determined by the projected Hamiltonian's symmetry.

This is not merely another instance of the framework being "insufficient" or "blind to structural content." It is a case where the framework's qualitative description is *actively misleading*: the framework would classify a first-order transition as lacking criticality (no saddle point, discontinuous jump), yet the physical system exhibits genuine criticality at the spinodal — with measurable Kibble-Zurek scaling — that is invisible to the full landscape's topology. The framework's Attractor primitive operates on the wrong mathematical object (the full Hamiltonian's landscape instead of the emergent subspace's landscape) and therefore makes a qualitatively wrong prediction (no criticality in first-order transitions).

The mechanism — resonant local excitations dynamically decoupling a subspace with emergent translational symmetry, followed by projection producing an effective Hamiltonian with a genuine critical point — involves three operations (resonant selection, Hilbert space projection, effective Hamiltonian construction) that are each individually beyond the framework's vocabulary. Together, they demonstrate that the physically relevant attractor landscape is not given *a priori* by the system's Hamiltonian but is *dynamically constructed* through spectral resonance and subspace decoupling. The framework has no concept of dynamically constructed attractor landscapes.

Paper 1 produces a secondary but sharp strain: the gravitational field's two contributions (self-field and pair potential) have categorically different information-theoretic consequences (Schmidt spectrum preservation vs. entanglement generation) despite both being "gradient descent in the curvature field." The framework's Field primitive treats all gravitational effects as instances of the same gradient, but the physical distinction between self-interaction (nonlinear, entanglement-preserving) and pair interaction (linear in pair coordinates, entanglement-generating) is a structural property of the gravitational computation that the gradient equation cannot express.

**T142 — Dynamically emergent attractor landscapes: the physically relevant landscape is not given by the Hamiltonian but is dynamically constructed through Hilbert-space decoupling**

Opened: this cycle

Description: Zhang et al. (2605.06436) demonstrate that first-order quantum phase transitions generically contain emergent second-order quantum critical points at the quantum spinodal, where metastability vanishes. The critical point exists not in the full Hamiltonian's landscape but in a projected effective Hamiltonian obtained by dynamically decoupling a resonant Hilbert subspace with emergent discrete translational symmetry. The full landscape has a first-order transition (discontinuous, no criticality); the emergent subspace landscape has a genuine second-order critical point with universality class, scaling exponents, and Kibble-Zurek dynamics.

The framework's Attractor primitive describes the attractor landscape as a property of the system's potential/energy surface — a fixed feature of the Hamiltonian whose topology determines phase transition character. Paper 2 shows the physically relevant landscape is *dynamically constructed* through three operations the framework cannot express: (a) identification of resonant local excitations at the spinodal; (b) dynamical decoupling of the corresponding Hilbert subspace (emergence of a conserved subspace via spectral resonance); (c) projection of the Hamiltonian onto this subspace, producing an effective Hamiltonian with qualitatively different topology (second-order critical point where the full Hamiltonian has only a first-order transition). The emergent translational symmetry in the subspace is absent in the full Hamiltonian — it is created by the decoupling process, not inherited from the microscopic theory.

This is distinct from T015 (resolution dynamics not determined by attractor structure alone, which concerns the quench protocol's role) because T142 concerns the *identity* of the relevant attractor landscape itself. It is distinct from T049 (scale-dependent symmetry type) because the emergent symmetry here is not produced by coarse-graining/RG flow but by dynamical Hilbert-space decoupling at a specific energy. It is distinct from T017 (continuous sharpening of criticality) because the emergent critical point is a genuine phase transition in the projected Hamiltonian, not a finite-size precursor. It is distinct from T112 (complexity phase transitions in resolution) because T142 concerns the *existence* of an emergent critical point within a first-order transition, not the computational difficulty of finding ground states. The prediction that the PXP model lacks spinodal criticality (because the resonant subspace there has no translational symmetry) demonstrates that the framework would need to derive the algebraic/symmetry properties of decoupled subspaces to predict which first-order transitions contain hidden criticality — an algebraic analysis far beyond any primitive.

Why unresolved: The framework would need to either (a) extend the Attractor primitive to include dynamically emergent landscapes — attractor topologies that exist only within projected subspaces selected by spectral resonance — which requires specifying when decoupling occurs, which subspaces emerge, and what symmetries they possess, all determined by spectral properties of the Hamiltonian no primitive can express; (b) develop a theory of Hilbert-space projection as a computational operation that transforms attractor landscapes, connecting to T057 (meta-computational primitives) but in a dynamical rather than theory-level sense — projection is a dynamical event that creates a new computational arena, not an operation on the theory itself; (c) abandon the claim that the attractor landscape is a fixed property of the Hamiltonian and replace it with "the relevant attractor landscape is dynamically selected by the system's spectral resonance structure," which would require the Attractor primitive to depend on the system's spectral properties — structural content no primitive can express (T026). The generality of the claim — "FOQPT dynamics is usually governed by an emergent critical point around the quantum spinodal point" — means this is not a special case but a generic feature of first-order quantum transitions, making the framework's silence on it a systematic gap.
