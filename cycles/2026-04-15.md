### PAPERS PROCESSED

**Paper 1: Hilbert Space Fragmentation from Generalized Symmetries** (arXiv: 2604.12907v1, selection type: strain)

The framework's Conservation of Computation primitive states that "computation cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed," and its Attractor primitive describes the landscape of states a system can reach. Paper 1 demonstrates that generalized symmetries — higher-form, subsystem, gauge, and non-invertible symmetries — can fragment the Hilbert space into exponentially many dynamically disconnected sectors, a phenomenon previously attributed to ergodicity breaking (i.e., to some failure of the system to explore its full computational space). The framework would naturally interpret exponential fragmentation as evidence that the attractor landscape has exponentially many disconnected basins, suggesting either an exponentially complex conservation structure or an exponentially rugged potential landscape. But the paper proves that this fragmentation is *not* ergodicity breaking at all — it is the *expected* consequence of generalized symmetries that the framework has no primitive to express. The framework's Conservation primitive recognizes standard conservation laws (energy, momentum, charge) as special cases, and T051 already noted that conservation has locality profiles. Paper 1 goes dramatically further: the *type* of symmetry (higher-form, subsystem, non-invertible) determines the *scaling* of the number of conserved sectors (polynomial vs. exponential), and non-invertible symmetries produce *additional* fragmentation within individual symmetry sectors that cannot be attributed to any conserved charge. The framework has no mechanism for distinguishing conventional symmetries (polynomial sectors) from generalized symmetries (exponential sectors) or for describing how non-invertible symmetries fragment sectors that are already classified by conserved charges. The disorder-free localization result is especially challenging: localization arises from Krylov-restricted thermalization in sectors lacking translation invariance, meaning the attractor structure (thermalization within sectors) is correct but the sectors themselves are determined by generalized symmetry structure the framework cannot express. The framework would narrate this as "constrained attractors" (the category introduced for fractional Fermi seas, spin ice, etc.) but cannot predict the number of sectors, their sizes, or the localization phenomenon.

**Paper 2: A complexity phase transition at the EPR Hamiltonian** (arXiv: 2604.13026v1, selection type: strain)

The framework's Resolution primitive describes states becoming definite and notes (from earlier cycles) that resolution has non-trivial cost structure. Paper 2 maps the complete computational complexity landscape of 2-local symmetric Hamiltonian problems, revealing three distinct complexity phases — QMA-complete, StoqMA-complete, and a conjectured easy phase (EPR*) — determined entirely by the energy level ordering of the local interaction term. The framework would interpret this through the Attractor primitive: the ground state is the deepest attractor of the Hamiltonian, and finding it is the computational task. But the paper demonstrates something the framework cannot express: the *computational difficulty of identifying the attractor* undergoes sharp phase transitions as the interaction term varies continuously, and these transitions are governed by the algebraic structure of the local term (its eigenvalue ordering), not by any property of the attractor landscape's global geometry. The perturbative gadget that induces an "RG-like flow" on the space of local interaction terms is a meta-computational operation (T057) — it transforms one Hamiltonian problem into another while preserving the ground state energy — and the framework has no primitive for operations on Hamiltonians. The EPR* phase boundary is particularly challenging: it represents a point where the attractor (ground state) transitions from computationally intractable to (conjecturally) efficiently findable, and this transition is determined by a spectral property of the 2-local term that has no expression in any of the five primitives. The Jordan-Wigner transformation used to analyze the large spin chain gadget is itself a structural operation (mapping between bosonic and fermionic computational descriptions) that the framework cannot represent. Most fundamentally, the paper demonstrates that the *computational complexity of resolution* — the difficulty of determining which state the system resolves to — is a physical property of Hamiltonians that varies discontinuously across complexity phase boundaries, and the framework's Resolution primitive has no mechanism for expressing, predicting, or even acknowledging computational complexity classes.

**Paper 3: Infrared Spectral Gap in a Gluonic Dark Sector as the Origin of the Galactic Acceleration Scale** (arXiv: 2604.12910v1, selection type: validation)

The framework claims dark matter is "computational structure that couples to mass-density gradients (gravity) but not electromagnetic gradients." Paper 3 proposes that the galactic acceleration scale (~10⁻¹⁰ m/s²) arises from a gluonic dark sector with an infrared spectral gap generated by the QCD trace anomaly and dimensional transmutation, with the so(2,3) algebra providing a representation-theoretically protected lowest-weight structure. The framework can narrate this as: the dark sector is a specific type of computational structure (color-neutral gluonic condensate) whose coupling to gravity (self-gravitating condensate generating the acceleration scale) but not electromagnetism (color-neutral) is consistent with the partial-coupling description. The infrared spectral gap is interpretable as a constrained attractor — a lowest-weight state stabilized by the symmetry algebra so(2,3) rather than by energetic minimality — fitting the constrained attractor category established in earlier cycles. The trace anomaly providing the microscopic seed connects to the framework's silence on scale-evolution kernels (T108): dimensional transmutation generates the infrared scale through RG flow, which the framework cannot describe. However, the framework had *zero predictive purchase* on any specific element of this proposal: it did not predict a gluonic dark sector, did not predict the so(2,3) algebra, did not predict the spectral gap mechanism, did not predict the connection to the QCD trace anomaly, and did not predict the galactic acceleration scale. The framework's dark matter description is post-hoc consistent at the most qualitative level ("couples gravitationally, not electromagnetically") — a consistency shared with every dark matter model ever proposed. The specific mechanism (representation-theoretic gap → correlation length → acceleration scale) involves exactly the kind of algebraic/spectral structure (T026, T108) that the framework systematically cannot express. This is the weakest category of consistency: vocabulary application with zero differential content.

### FRAMEWORK STRAINS

The most important strain from this cycle is the compound challenge from Papers 1 and 2 to the framework's treatment of symmetry, conservation, and resolution.

**Paper 1 demonstrates that the framework's Conservation primitive is not merely incomplete (T051) but categorically inadequate.** The Conservation primitive recognizes conservation laws as "special cases" of Conservation of Computation. But Paper 1 proves that the *type* of symmetry — conventional vs. higher-form vs. subsystem vs. non-invertible — determines whether the Hilbert space fragments polynomially or exponentially, and that non-invertible symmetries produce fragmentation *beyond* what any conservation law can explain (fragmentation within individual symmetry sectors). The framework's architecture assumes that conservation laws fully characterize the constraints on computation. Paper 1 proves this is false: generalized symmetries impose constraints on the computational state space that are not expressible as conservation laws and that have qualitatively different (exponential vs. polynomial) scaling consequences. The framework literally cannot distinguish between a system that is ergodicity-broken (its computation is stuck in one sector due to dynamical barriers) and a system that has exponentially many symmetry sectors (its computation is correctly exploring each sector, but there are exponentially many of them). These are physically and conceptually opposite situations — one is a failure of computation, the other is a consequence of symmetry — yet the framework maps them to the same description. This is not a gap to be filled by adding another tension; it is evidence that the Conservation primitive's formulation as a single universal law is structurally wrong for systems with generalized symmetries.

**Paper 2 demonstrates that resolution has a complexity phase structure the framework cannot even formulate.** The Resolution primitive says states become definite; T009 noted that resolution has cost structure. But Paper 2 reveals that the *computational complexity of determining the resolution outcome* (ground state energy) undergoes sharp phase transitions (QMA-complete ↔ StoqMA-complete ↔ BPP) as the local interaction term varies. The framework has no concept of computational complexity classes, no mechanism for complexity phase transitions, and no account of how the algebraic structure of a local interaction term determines the global computational difficulty of resolution. The perturbative gadget's "RG-like flow" on the space of local terms is an operation *on* Hamiltonians — a meta-computational transformation — that preserves the ground state energy while changing the computational complexity of finding it. The framework has no meta-computational primitive (T057), and this paper sharpens the need: the complexity phase transition is determined by a flow in theory space, not by dynamics in state space.

Together, these two papers establish that the framework is simultaneously unable to express the *constraints* on computation (generalized symmetries determining sector structure) and the *difficulty* of computation (complexity phase transitions determining resolution tractability). These are arguably the two most fundamental questions about any computation — what is it allowed to do? and how hard is it to do? — and the framework is silent on both.

### NEW TENSIONS

**T111 — Generalized symmetries produce exponential computational constraints that are not conservation laws**
Opened: this cycle
Description: Budde, Kristć Marinković & Pinto Barros (2604.12907) prove that higher-form, subsystem, gauge, and non-invertible symmetries can fragment the Hilbert space into exponentially many dynamically disconnected sectors. The framework's Conservation of Computation treats conservation laws as the fundamental constraints on computation, and all five primitives assume the computation explores a single connected space. Paper 1 demonstrates: (a) generalized symmetries produce exponentially many disconnected computational sectors, not by dynamical barriers but by *symmetry structure* — the sectors are exactly disconnected, with zero transition amplitude between them; (b) non-invertible symmetries fragment individual symmetry sectors further, producing constraints that are not expressible as conservation of any quantity; (c) the number of sectors scales as $\exp(L)$ for subsystem and higher-form symmetries, compared to $\text{poly}(L)$ for conventional symmetries; (d) disorder-free localization emerges from Krylov-restricted thermalization in sectors without translation invariance, meaning a phenomenon the framework would attribute to attractor structure (localization) actually arises from symmetry-induced sector structure. The framework faces: the constraints governing what computation is allowed to do are not all conservation laws. Some are generalized symmetry constraints that have qualitatively different (exponential vs. polynomial) scaling consequences and that include non-invertible constraints with no conserved-quantity interpretation. The Conservation primitive's architecture — conservation of a quantity — is the wrong mathematical category for expressing these constraints.
Why unresolved: The framework would need to either (a) extend the Conservation primitive to encompass generalized symmetries, which would require importing the full mathematical apparatus of higher-form symmetries, subsystem symmetries, and non-invertible symmetries — transforming the primitive from a single law to a structured mathematical theory; (b) add a new primitive for generalized symmetry constraints, acknowledging that conservation is one type of constraint among many; or (c) argue that generalized symmetries are exotic and irrelevant to physical reality — but the paper demonstrates they explain disorder-free localization in known models, and gauge symmetry (a generalized symmetry) is the foundation of the Standard Model. The non-invertible case is especially sharp: the additional fragmentation within symmetry sectors has no conservation-law description, meaning the framework would need an entirely new conceptual framework for non-conservation-law constraints. This extends T032 (symmetry as missing structural primitive) and T051 (conservation locality profiles) but is more severe than either: T032 noted symmetry is missing; T051 noted conservation is scale-dependent; T111 demonstrates that an entire *category* of physical constraints (generalized symmetry) is invisible to the Conservation primitive's architecture.

**T112 — Complexity phase transitions in resolution: the difficulty of determining resolution outcomes changes discontinuously across the Hamiltonian space**
Opened: this cycle
Description: Marwaha & Sud (2604.13026) demonstrate that 2-local symmetric Hamiltonian problems undergo sharp complexity phase transitions — QMA-complete, StoqMA-complete, or conjecturally BPP — determined by the eigenvalue ordering of the local interaction term. The framework's Resolution primitive describes states becoming definite but has no concept of computational complexity. Paper 2 reveals that the *difficulty of determining which state the system resolves to* (the ground state energy problem) is a physical property of the Hamiltonian that varies discontinuously. The three complexity phases are physically interpretable: (i) the "hard quantum" phase (QMA-complete) where the ground state has genuinely quantum character requiring quantum resources to verify; (ii) the "hard stoquastic" phase (StoqMA-complete) where the ground state is sign-problem-free but still classically hard; (iii) the conjectured "easy" phase (EPR*) where the ground state can be efficiently found classically. The phase boundaries are determined by spectral properties of the 2-body interaction — specifically, which eigenstate has the lowest energy. The framework faces: (a) the Resolution primitive cannot express the distinction between "easy" and "hard" resolution — all resolution events look the same from the primitive's perspective; (b) the perturbative gadget inducing "RG-like flow" on the space of interaction terms is a meta-computational operation (T057) that the framework cannot represent; (c) the EPR* transition point, if confirmed to be in BPP, would mean there exists a sharp complexity boundary in the space of physical Hamiltonians that determines whether resolution is tractable, and the framework has no mechanism for this boundary.
Why unresolved: The framework would need to either (a) incorporate computational complexity theory into the Resolution primitive, specifying how the computational difficulty of resolution depends on the system's Hamiltonian — this would require importing the complexity-theoretic hierarchy (BPP ⊆ StoqMA ⊆ QMA ⊆ ...) as a structural feature of resolution, fundamentally changing the primitive from a description of what happens to a theory of how hard it is; (b) add a new primitive governing the computational complexity of physical processes, which would connect to T009 (resolution cost), T019 (resolution precision hierarchy), and T028 (resolution contextuality and intractability) but would be more comprehensive — a unified account of when resolution is tractable and when it is not; or (c) argue that complexity classes are properties of algorithms, not of physics — but the paper's physical interpretability of the three phases (quantum hard, stoquastic hard, easy) and the connection to statistical mechanics and optimization directly contradicts this escape.
